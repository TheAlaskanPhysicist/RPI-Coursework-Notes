We often have systems who use commutators and anticommutators. However, it is interesting that there is effectively a phase difference between them dependent usually on the spin of a particle. Suppose that we have a new commutator of the form
$$\begin{align}
\left[A,B\right]_{s}&=AB-e^{2\pi is}BA
\end{align}$$
For bosons and fermions, we correctly recover
$$\begin{align}
\left[A,B\right]_{\mathbb{Z}}&=\left[A,B\right] & \left[A,B\right]_{\mathbb{Z}+\tfrac{1}{2}}&=\left\{A,B\right\}
\end{align}$$
While interesting by itself, I believe that this is relatively nonsense in the specific extension. However, what we can notice is that the standard commutators we use preserve that
$$\begin{align}
A&\in F_1 & B&\in F_2 &\implies&& \left[A,B\right],\left\{A,B\right\}\in F_1\times F_2
\end{align}$$
The exponential above also just happens to be an element of $\mathrm{SU}(1)$. The interesting thing to note is that the period of the complex argument to repeat shows up as a multiplicative factor on the Lie Algebra element, while the spin $s$ encodes an effective half twist that changes the full multiplication from a $2\pi$ cycle to a $4\pi$ cycle.
